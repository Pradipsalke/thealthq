In talent, shape matters

Rethinking the talent pyramid and the hidden cost of mediocrity

Nilesh Biniwale and Nilesh Kulkarni An excerpt from The ALT HQ · 4 min read

“To really nurture talent and skill development,” says Nilesh Biniwale, “we need to understand the ‘Talent Pyramid’. It’s not just a nice diagram. It’s the foundation of how successful organisations actually build their capabilities.”

Nilesh Kulkarni agrees wholeheartedly: “The classic talent pyramid needs to be used for overall talent strategy. We’ve seen too many GCCs ignore this model and pay the price later.”

In building a sustainable and high-performing organisation, especially within GCCs, talent strategy must be approached with as much discipline and clarity as product strategy. One timeless model that still holds immense value is the classic talent pyramid, but only when implemented thoughtfully.

At the base of this pyramid are the Feeders: early-career professionals, often from campuses or finishing schools, who bring fresh energy, curiosity, and disruptive thinking. They operate at a lower salary band but are a vital source of innovation. Empowering this group, giving them ownership and real-world problems to solve, accelerates not just delivery but also cultural vitality. This layer challenges the norm and keeps the system dynamic.

Next come the Stabilizers: mid-level professionals with experience and maturity. They don’t just follow processes. They enforce them, refine them, and act as the organisation’s shock absorbers. When Feeders propose bold ideas, Stabilizers assess the risk, validate feasibility, and ensure responsible execution. Their role is to institutionalise what’s promising and protect the organisation from what’s not. They strengthen the core.

At the top of the pyramid are the Differentiators: strategic drivers who are few in number but critical in impact. They break down macro objectives into micro plans. They turn vision into executable tasks. Often more expensive, they’re expected to deliver exponentially higher value.

Every value stream in an organisation should reflect this triangular talent structure. Overlaying this, there exists another dimension, performance and potential: low, mid, and high. Ideally, the Differentiator layer should only contain high-potential and high-performing individuals. But mistakes happen, and sometimes, external hires who haven’t been rigorously evaluated enter this layer and dilute its quality.

This misalignment is where problems begin; and over time, if left unchecked, leads to a dangerous organisational pattern: the apple-shaped talent pyramid.

The apple-shaped pyramid: a silent regressor

When organisations fail to nurture high potential talent, and instead accumulate average performers, the healthy pyramid begins to distort:

The base narrows as fewer Feeders are hired or empowered. Innovation dries up. The talent supply chain weakens.

The middle bulges with an excess of Stabilizers, many of whom are average performers. This is the “apple” part, fat in the centre, where career growth stalls, energy dips, and risk aversion sets in.

The top gets diluted, with Differentiators who may be misaligned or not high-performing, often due to flawed hiring or internal misjudgements.

In such a model, costs rise; tenure increases compensation but not impact. Decision-making slows. Fresh ideas struggle to reach execution. High-potential employees feel stifled and leave. Bottom performers are eventually let go. But what’s left behind is a bloated middle layer that defines the new normal. Mediocrity becomes the standard.

The greatest threat to a high-performance culture isn’t low performers, it’s the entrenchment of average talent in key layers. Over time, this drives the organisation from excellence to stagnation.

Breaking the apple shape: a call for talent precision

To fix this drift, organisations must act with intent:

Revitalise the base (Feeders)

  • Build strong university and early-career programmes.
  • Create real ownership paths for young talent.
  • Recognise innovation from the bottom, not just the top.

Right-size the middle (Stabilizers)

  • Differentiate between average and emerging leaders.
  • Run upskilling or re-deployment programmes for the willing.
  • Create exit paths for persistent underperformers and mediocre performers.

Sharpen the top (Differentiators)

  • Promote based on capability, not comfort or politics.
  • Provide Differentiators with cross-functional stretch roles.
  • Ensure accountability, not just compensation.

Measure the shape, not just the score

  • Conduct regular talent health diagnostics.
  • Review potential and performance matrices as rigorously as financial reports.
  • Challenge whether the pyramid is still a pyramid, or if it’s slowly becoming an apple.

Final thought

Talent management is not just about removing the weakest links. It’s about identifying the strongest, and building systems that enable them to rise. Inaction on top talent is more dangerous than the presence of low performers.

As leaders, we must teach our managers to spot potential, invest in it, and avoid the comfort trap of mediocrity. GCCs, in particular, cannot afford an apple-shaped structure. They must stay lean at the top, bold at the base, and agile in the middle.

In talent, shape matters. And the healthiest shape is one that grows from the bottom, fortifies the middle, and sharpens the top.
